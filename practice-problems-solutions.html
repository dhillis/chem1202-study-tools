Chapter 16 — Kinetics
P1Reaction Rate from Stoichiometry

2N2O5(g) → 4NO2(g) + O2(g). [N2O5] drops from 0.500 M to 0.480 M over 20.0 s. Find: (a) rate of N2O5 disappearance, (b) rate of reaction, (c) rate of O2 formation, (d) rate of NO2 formation.

Strategy: First find the raw rate of change for the species you're given. Then divide by that species' coefficient (with a minus sign for a reactant) to get the single overall "rate of reaction." Finally, multiply that rate of reaction by each other species' own coefficient (plus sign for products, minus for reactants) to get its individual rate.
  1. Rate of N2O5 disappearance = −Δ[N2O5]/Δt = −(0.480−0.500)/20.0 = −(−0.020)/20.0 = 1.0×10−3 M/s.
  2. Rate of reaction = (1/2) × rate of N2O5 disappearance (dividing by its coefficient, 2) = (1/2)(1.0×10−3) = 5.0×10−4 M/s.
  3. Rate of O2 formation = (1) × rate of reaction (O2's coefficient is 1) = 5.0×10−4 M/s.
  4. Rate of NO2 formation = (4) × rate of reaction (NO2's coefficient is 4) = 4 × 5.0×10−4 = 2.0×10−3 M/s.
(a) 1.0×10−3 M/s   (b) 5.0×10−4 M/s   (c) 5.0×10−4 M/s   (d) 2.0×10−3 M/s
P2Method of Initial Rates

2A + B → products. Trial 1: [A]=0.20, [B]=0.10, rate=4.0×10−3. Trial 2: [A]=0.40, [B]=0.10, rate=1.6×10−2. Trial 3: [A]=0.20, [B]=0.20, rate=8.0×10−3.

Strategy: Find a pair of trials where only ONE concentration changes. Compute the ratio of rates and the ratio of that concentration; solve (conc. ratio)order = (rate ratio) for the order — this is an exponent equation, so don't just divide the ratios. Repeat for the other reactant. Then plug any one trial into the completed rate law to solve for k.
  1. Order in A (compare trials 1→2: [A] doubles, [B] constant): rate ratio = (1.6×10−2)/(4.0×10−3) = 4.0. Solve 2n = 4.0 → n = 2 (second order in A).
  2. Order in B (compare trials 1→3: [B] doubles, [A] constant): rate ratio = (8.0×10−3)/(4.0×10−3) = 2.0. Solve 2m = 2.0 → m = 1 (first order in B).
  3. Overall order = n + m = 2 + 1 = 3rd order.
  4. Rate law: Rate = k[A]2[B].
  5. Solve for k using Trial 1: 4.0×10−3 = k(0.20)2(0.10) = k(0.0040) → k = (4.0×10−3)/(0.0040) = 1.0. Units: since overall order is 3, k has units of M−2·s−1 (so that k[A]2[B] comes out in M/s).
n=2, m=1, overall order=3; Rate = k[A]²[B]; k = 1.0 M−2·s−1
P3First-Order Integrated Rate Law & Half-Life

First order in A, k = 0.0410 min−1, [A]0 = 0.640 M. Find [A] after 15.0 min, and the half-life.

Strategy: "First order" + "concentration at a later time" signals the first-order integrated rate law, [A]t = [A]0e−kt. For half-life, use t½ = ln2/k directly — it does NOT depend on [A]0 for first order, so you don't need the answer from part (a) to do part (b).
  1. [A]t = [A]0e−kt = 0.640 × e−(0.0410)(15.0) = 0.640 × e−0.615 = 0.640 × 0.5406.
  2. [A]t = 0.346 M.
  3. t½ = ln(2)/k = 0.6931/0.0410.
  4. t½ = 16.9 min.
[A] after 15.0 min = 0.346 M; t½ = 16.9 min
P4Second-Order Integrated Rate Law & Half-Life

Second order in A, k = 0.0360 L/mol·min, [A]0 = 0.250 M. Find [A] after 40.0 min, and the half-life.

Strategy: "Second order" signals 1/[A]t = kt + 1/[A]0. Solve for 1/[A]t first, then invert. For half-life, use t½ = 1/(k[A]0) — this one DOES depend on [A]0, unlike first order.
  1. 1/[A]t = kt + 1/[A]0 = (0.0360)(40.0) + 1/0.250 = 1.44 + 4.00 = 5.44.
  2. [A]t = 1/5.44 = 0.184 M.
  3. t½ = 1/(k[A]0) = 1/[(0.0360)(0.250)] = 1/0.00900.
  4. t½ = 111 min.
[A] after 40.0 min = 0.184 M; t½ = 111 min
P5Zero-Order Half-Life Pattern

Zero order in A, k = 0.0220 M/s, [A]0 = 0.880 M. Find the first and second half-lives, and compare them.

Strategy: For zero order, t½ = [A]0/(2k) — but this formula only gives the FIRST half-life directly. For the second half-life, treat the concentration at the end of the first half-life (0.440 M) as a NEW starting concentration and reapply the same formula.
  1. First half-life: t½ = [A]0/(2k) = 0.880/(2 × 0.0220) = 0.880/0.0440 = 20.0 s (takes [A] from 0.880 to 0.440 M).
  2. Second half-life: reapply the formula with the new starting concentration, 0.440 M: t½ = 0.440/0.0440 = 10.0 s (takes [A] from 0.440 to 0.220 M).
  3. The second half-life (10.0 s) is exactly HALF the first (20.0 s) — this is the general zero-order pattern: each successive half-life is half as long as the one before, because t½ is directly proportional to the (shrinking) starting concentration.
First half-life = 20.0 s; second half-life = 10.0 s (half as long, as expected for zero order)
P6Arrhenius Equation — Solving for Ea

k1 = 2.0×10−3 at T1 = 295 K; k2 = 8.9×10−2 at T2 = 350 K. Find Ea.

Strategy: Two (k, T) pairs signal the two-point Arrhenius equation. Rearrange to solve for Ea directly — no need to convert temperature units here since both are already in Kelvin. Watch your sign: the equation has a minus sign built in.
  1. Start from ln(k2/k1) = −(Ea/R)(1/T2 − 1/T1). Rearranged for Ea: Ea = R·ln(k2/k1) ÷ (1/T1 − 1/T2).
  2. ln(k2/k1) = ln(8.9×10−2/2.0×10−3) = ln(44.5) = 3.795.
  3. 1/T1 − 1/T2 = 1/295 − 1/350 = 0.003390 − 0.002857 = 5.32×10−4 K−1.
  4. Ea = (8.314)(3.795) ÷ (5.32×10−4) = 31.55 ÷ 5.32×10−4 = 59,300 J/mol.
Ea ≈ 59.2 kJ/mol
P7Arrhenius Equation — Solving for k at a New Temperature

Ea = 88.0 kJ/mol; k1 = 5.0×10−4 at T1 = 310 K. Find k2 at T2 = 340 K.

Strategy: Given Ea and one (k, T) point, solve the two-point equation for ln(k2/k1) first, then exponentiate to isolate k2. Convert Ea to Joules to match R = 8.314 J/mol·K.
  1. Ea = 88.0 kJ/mol = 88,000 J/mol.
  2. ln(k2/k1) = −(Ea/R)(1/T2 − 1/T1) = −(88000/8.314)(1/340 − 1/310) = −(10,585)(−2.849×10−4) = 3.016.
  3. k2/k1 = e3.016 = 20.4.
  4. k2 = k1 × 20.4 = (5.0×10−4)(20.4).
k2 ≈ 1.0×10−2 (same units as k1)
P8Reaction Mechanism Analysis

Step 1 (slow): NO2Cl(g) → NO2(g) + Cl(g). Step 2 (fast): NO2Cl(g) + Cl(g) → NO2(g) + Cl2(g).

Strategy: Add the steps and cancel anything that appears as both a product and a reactant (that's your intermediate) to get the overall equation. Molecularity = count of reactant particles in EACH individual step. The rate law comes only from the slow (rate-determining) step, using its own coefficients as orders (valid since it's elementary).
  1. Overall equation: add the two steps: NO2Cl + NO2Cl + Cl → NO2 + Cl + NO2 + Cl2. Cl cancels (appears on both sides): 2NO2Cl(g) → 2NO2(g) + Cl2(g).
  2. Molecularity: Step 1 has 1 reactant particle (NO2Cl) → unimolecular. Step 2 has 2 reactant particles (NO2Cl and Cl) → bimolecular.
  3. Intermediate: Cl is produced in step 1 and consumed in step 2 (and doesn't appear in the overall equation) → Cl is the intermediate.
  4. Rate law: the rate-determining step is step 1 (slow), which is elementary and unimolecular, so its rate law uses its own coefficient: Rate = k1[NO2Cl].
Overall: 2NO2Cl(g) → 2NO2(g) + Cl2(g); Step 1 unimolecular, Step 2 bimolecular; intermediate = Cl; Rate = k[NO2Cl]
P9Reaction Energy Diagram

Ea(forward) = 62 kJ/mol; ΔHrxn = −35 kJ/mol.

Strategy: The sign of ΔH tells you exo/endothermic directly. Use ΔHrxn = Ea(fwd) − Ea(rev), rearranged to solve for Ea(rev).
  1. ΔHrxn = −35 kJ/mol is negative → exothermic (products lower in energy than reactants).
  2. Rearrange: Ea(rev) = Ea(fwd) − ΔHrxn = 62 − (−35) = 62 + 35.
  3. Ea(rev) = 97 kJ/mol.
  4. Ea(rev) (97) > Ea(fwd) (62) — this makes sense: since the reaction is exothermic, the products sit in a lower energy well than the reactants, so climbing back UP from products to the transition state (the reverse reaction) requires crossing a bigger energy gap than going forward.
Exothermic; Ea(reverse) = 97 kJ/mol (larger than Ea(forward), as expected for an exothermic reaction)
Chapter 17 — Equilibrium
P10Writing K Expressions & Interpreting Magnitude

(a) 2NO(g) + O2(g) ⇆ 2NO2(g), Kc = 6.0×105. (b) Ag2CO3(s) ⇆ 2Ag+(aq) + CO32−(aq).

Strategy: Products over reactants, each raised to its coefficient. Omit any pure solid or pure liquid. Compare K to 1: K » 1 favors products, K « 1 favors reactants.
  1. (a) Kc = [NO2]2 / ([NO]2[O2]). Since 6.0×105 is much greater than 1, the equilibrium strongly favors products.
  2. (b) Ag2CO3(s) is a pure solid and is omitted. Kc = [Ag+]2[CO32−] (note: this is why a "solubility product" expression has no denominator — the only solid drops out entirely).
(a) Kc = [NO2]²/([NO]²[O2]); favors products. (b) Kc = [Ag+]²[CO32−]
P11Reaction Quotient (Q vs. K)

K = 170 for 2NO2(g) ⇆ N2O4(g). At some point, [NO2] = 0.025 M, [N2O4] = 0.040 M.

Strategy: Write Q using the exact same form as K, plug in the given (non-equilibrium) concentrations, then compare Q to K to determine direction.
  1. Q = [N2O4]/[NO2]2 = 0.040/(0.025)2 = 0.040/0.000625.
  2. Q = 64.
  3. Compare: Q (64) < K (170), so the reaction is NOT at equilibrium and must proceed toward products (right) to raise Q up to 170.
Q = 64; not at equilibrium; shifts right (toward N2O4)
P12Converting Kc to Kp

2SO3(g) ⇆ 2SO2(g) + O2(g), Kc = 4.08×10−3 at T = 1000 K.

Strategy: Find Δngas (product gas moles minus reactant gas moles) from the balanced equation, then plug into Kp = Kc(RT)Δn using R = 0.0821 L·atm/mol·K.
  1. Δngas = (2 + 1) − 2 = +1.
  2. RT = (0.0821)(1000) = 82.1.
  3. Kp = Kc(RT)Δn = (4.08×10−3)(82.1)1 = (4.08×10−3)(82.1).
Kp ≈ 0.335
P13ICE Table — Perfect-Square Shortcut

H2(g) + Br2(g) ⇆ 2HBr(g), K = 81.0. 3.00 L flask: 1.20 mol H2, 1.20 mol Br2, 0 mol HBr.

Strategy: Convert moles to molarity first. Since [H2]0 = [Br2]0, the K expression becomes a perfect square — take a square root instead of using the full quadratic formula.
  1. Initial concentrations: [H2]0 = [Br2]0 = 1.20 mol ÷ 3.00 L = 0.400 M.
  2. ICE table: H2: 0.400−x; Br2: 0.400−x; HBr: 0 + 2x.
  3. K = (2x)2 / [(0.400−x)(0.400−x)] = [2x/(0.400−x)]2 = 81.0.
  4. Take the square root of both sides: 2x/(0.400−x) = √81.0 = 9.00.
  5. Solve: 2x = 9.00(0.400−x) = 3.60 − 9.00x → 11.00x = 3.60 → x = 0.327 M.
  6. [H2]eq = [Br2]eq = 0.400 − 0.327 = 0.0727 M. [HBr]eq = 2(0.327) = 0.655 M.
  7. Check: K = (0.655)2/[(0.0727)(0.0727)] = 0.429/0.00529 ≈ 81.1 ✓ (matches, within rounding).
[H2]eq = [Br2]eq = 0.0727 M; [HBr]eq = 0.655 M
P14ICE Table — Small-x Approximation

2SO2(g) + O2(g) ⇆ 2SO3(g), Kp = 1.0×10−3. PSO2 = 4.0 atm, PO2 = 2.5 atm, PSO3 = 0 initially.

Strategy: Since Kp is small, assume x is small enough that (initial − x) ≈ initial for the reactants — this avoids a cubic/quadratic. ALWAYS check afterward that x is less than about 5% of the smallest initial value.
  1. ICE (pressures): SO2: 4.0−2x ≈ 4.0; O2: 2.5−x ≈ 2.5; SO3: 0+2x.
  2. Kp = (2x)2 / [(4.0)2(2.5)] = 1.0×10−3.
  3. (2x)2 = (1.0×10−3)(16)(2.5) = 0.040.
  4. 2x = √0.040 = 0.200 → x = 0.100 atm.
  5. PSO3 = 2x = 0.20 atm.
  6. Check the approximation: x (0.100) compared to the SMALLER initial pressure (O2 = 2.5 atm): 0.100/2.5 = 4.0%. Since this is under the usual 5% cutoff, the approximation is valid.
PSO3 ≈ 0.20 atm; approximation valid (x is 4.0% of the limiting initial pressure)
P15Calculating Kp from Equilibrium Pressures

N2(g) + 3H2(g) ⇆ 2NH3(g). At equilibrium: PN2=0.50 atm, PH2=0.75 atm, PNH3=0.20 atm.

Strategy: When you're already given equilibrium pressures directly, just plug straight into the Kp expression — no ICE table needed.
  1. Kp = PNH32 / (PN2·PH23) = (0.20)2 / [(0.50)(0.75)3].
  2. Numerator: 0.0400. Denominator: 0.50 × 0.4219 = 0.2109.
  3. Kp = 0.0400/0.2109.
Kp ≈ 0.190
P16Le Châtelier's Principle — Multiple Reactions

Predict the shift when volume is decreased (pressure increased) for four reactions, plus a temperature question for a fifth.

Strategy: Count total moles of GAS ONLY on each side. Compressing (raising pressure) always shifts toward the side with FEWER gas moles; if both sides have equal gas moles, there's no shift. For temperature, treat heat as a reactant (endothermic) or product (exothermic).
  1. (a) 2NO(g) + O2(g) ⇆ 2NO2(g): reactant side = 2+1 = 3 mol gas; product side = 2 mol gas. Fewer moles on product side → shifts right.
  2. (b) H2(g) + CO2(g) ⇆ H2O(g) + CO(g): 1+1=2 mol gas on both sides → no shift.
  3. (c) N2O4(g) ⇆ 2NO2(g): reactant side = 1 mol gas; product side = 2 mol gas. Fewer moles on reactant side → shifts left.
  4. (d) CaCO3(s) ⇆ CaO(s) + CO2(g): only count gas — reactant side = 0 mol gas (CaCO3 is solid); product side = 1 mol gas (CO2). Fewer moles on reactant side → shifts left (favors more solid CaCO3).
  5. (e) 2SO2(g) + O2(g) ⇆ 2SO3(g) is exothermic — treat heat as a product (reactants ⇆ products + energy). Raising temperature is like adding more "product" (energy), so the equilibrium shifts left (toward reactants), and K decreases.
(a) right   (b) no shift   (c) left   (d) left   (e) shifts left; K decreases
