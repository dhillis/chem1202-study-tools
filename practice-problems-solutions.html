P1Reaction Rate from Stoichiometry

2N2O5(g) → 4NO2(g) + O2(g). [N2O5] drops from 0.500 M to 0.480 M over 20.0 s. Find: (a) rate of N2O5 disappearance, (b) rate of reaction, (c) rate of O2 formation, (d) rate of NO2 formation.

Strategy: First find the raw rate of change for the species you're given. Then divide by that species' coefficient (with a minus sign for a reactant) to get the single overall "rate of reaction." Finally, multiply that rate of reaction by each other species' own coefficient (plus sign for products, minus for reactants) to get its individual rate.
  1. Write the general rate expression relating every species to the single "rate of reaction," dividing each term by its own coefficient (reactants get a minus sign):
    Rate = −12Δ[N2O5]Δt = 14Δ[NO2]Δt = Δ[O2]Δt
  2. Identify the knowns: [N2O5]0 = 0.500 M, [N2O5]t = 0.480 M, Δt = 20.0 s.
  3. (a) Compute Δ[N2O5]/Δt directly from the data:
    Δ[N2O5]Δt = 0.480 − 0.50020.0 = −0.02020.0 = −1.0×10−3 M/s
    The rate of disappearance is the negative of this: 1.0×10−3 M/s.
  4. (b) Compute the rate of reaction by dividing by N2O5's coefficient (2):
    Rate = −12(−1.0×10−3 M/s) = 5.0×10−4 M/s
  5. (c) Compute the rate of O2 formation — O2's coefficient is 1, so its rate equals the rate of reaction directly: 5.0×10−4 M/s.
  6. (d) Compute the rate of NO2 formation — multiply the rate of reaction by NO2's coefficient (4):
    Δ[NO2]Δt = 4 × Rate = 4 × 5.0×10−4 M/s = 2.0×10−3 M/s
(a) 1.0×10−3 M/s   (b) 5.0×10−4 M/s   (c) 5.0×10−4 M/s   (d) 2.0×10−3 M/s
P2Method of Initial Rates

2A + B → products. Trial 1: [A]=0.20, [B]=0.10, rate=4.0×10−3. Trial 2: [A]=0.40, [B]=0.10, rate=1.6×10−2. Trial 3: [A]=0.20, [B]=0.20, rate=8.0×10−3. Determine (a) the order with respect to A, (b) the order with respect to B, (c) the overall reaction order, (d) the rate law, and (e) the value of k, with correct units.

Strategy: Find a pair of trials where only ONE concentration changes. Compute the ratio of rates and the ratio of that concentration; solve (conc. ratio)order = (rate ratio) for the order — this is an exponent equation, so don't just divide the ratios. Repeat for the other reactant. Then plug any one trial into the completed rate law to solve for k.
  1. Write the general rate law:
    Rate = k[A]n[B]m
  2. Find the order in A (n): choose Trials 1 and 2, where [B] stays constant but [A] doubles. Set up the ratio of the two rate laws:
    Rate2Rate1 = [A]2n[A]1n   ⇒   1.6×10−24.0×10−3 = 0.400.20n
  3. Simplify both sides: 4.0 = (2.0)n.
  4. Solve the exponent equation by inspection (or logs): since 2.02 = 4.0, n = 2 (second order in A).
  5. Find the order in B (m): choose Trials 1 and 3, where [A] stays constant but [B] doubles:
    8.0×10−34.0×10−3 = 0.200.10m   ⇒   2.0 = (2.0)m
    Since 2.01 = 2.0, m = 1 (first order in B).
  6. Add the individual orders to get the overall order: n + m = 2 + 1 = 3rd order overall.
  7. Write the completed rate law: Rate = k[A]2[B].
  8. Solve for k by substituting Trial 1's data into the completed rate law:
    4.0×10−3 = k(0.20)2(0.10) = k(0.040)(0.10) = k(0.0040)
    k = 4.0×10−30.0040 = 1.0
  9. Determine the units of k: since Rate (M/s) = k·M2·M = k·M3, k must carry units of M−2·s−1 to make the equation balance.
n=2, m=1, overall order=3; Rate = k[A]²[B]; k = 1.0 M−2·s−1
P3First-Order Integrated Rate Law & Half-Life

First order in A, k = 0.0410 min−1, [A]0 = 0.640 M. Find [A] after 15.0 min, and the half-life.

Strategy: "First order" + "concentration at a later time" signals the first-order integrated rate law, [A]t = [A]0e−kt. For half-life, use t½ = ln2/k directly — it does NOT depend on [A]0 for first order, so you don't need the answer from part (a) to do part (b).
  1. Write the first-order integrated rate law:
    [A]t = [A]0e−kt
  2. Identify the knowns: [A]0 = 0.640 M, k = 0.0410 min−1, t = 15.0 min.
  3. Compute the exponent first: −kt = −(0.0410)(15.0) = −0.615.
  4. Substitute into the integrated law:
    [A]t = 0.640 × e−0.615
  5. Evaluate the exponential: e−0.615 = 0.5406.
  6. Multiply to get the final concentration: [A]t = 0.640 × 0.5406 = 0.346 M.
  7. Write the first-order half-life equation (independent of [A]0):
    t½ = ln 2k
  8. Substitute and compute:
    t½ = 0.69310.0410 = 16.9 min
[A] after 15.0 min = 0.346 M; t½ = 16.9 min
P4Second-Order Integrated Rate Law & Half-Life

Second order in A, k = 0.0360 L/mol·min, [A]0 = 0.250 M. Find [A] after 40.0 min, and the half-life.

Strategy: "Second order" signals 1/[A]t = kt + 1/[A]0. Solve for 1/[A]t first, then invert. For half-life, use t½ = 1/(k[A]0) — this one DOES depend on [A]0, unlike first order.
  1. Write the second-order integrated rate law:
    1[A]t = kt + 1[A]0
  2. Identify the knowns: k = 0.0360 L/mol·min, t = 40.0 min, [A]0 = 0.250 M.
  3. Compute the kt term: kt = (0.0360)(40.0) = 1.44.
  4. Compute the 1/[A]0 term:
    1[A]0 = 10.250 = 4.00
  5. Add the two terms:
    1[A]t = 1.44 + 4.00 = 5.44
  6. Invert to solve for [A]t: [A]t = 1/5.44 = 0.184 M.
  7. Write the second-order half-life equation (this one depends on [A]0):
    t½ = 1k[A]0
  8. Substitute and compute:
    t½ = 1(0.0360)(0.250) = 10.00900 = 111 min
[A] after 40.0 min = 0.184 M; t½ = 111 min
P5Zero-Order Half-Life Pattern

Zero order in A, k = 0.0220 M/s, [A]0 = 0.880 M. Find the first and second half-lives, and compare them.

Strategy: For zero order, t½ = [A]0/(2k) — but this formula only gives the FIRST half-life directly. For the second half-life, treat the concentration at the end of the first half-life (0.440 M) as a NEW starting concentration and reapply the same formula.
  1. Write the zero-order half-life equation:
    t½ = [A]02k
  2. Compute the first half-life using the original [A]0 = 0.880 M:
    t½,1 = 0.8802(0.0220) = 0.8800.0440 = 20.0 s
    This takes [A] from 0.880 M down to 0.440 M.
  3. Set up the second half-life: the concentration remaining after the first half-life, 0.440 M, now becomes the NEW starting concentration for the next half-life calculation.
  4. Reapply the same formula with the new starting concentration:
    t½,2 = 0.4400.0440 = 10.0 s
    This takes [A] from 0.440 M down to 0.220 M.
  5. Compare the two half-lives:
    t½,2t½,1 = 10.020.0 = 0.500
    The second half-life is exactly HALF the first.
  6. Explain the pattern: because t½ is directly proportional to [A]0 for a zero-order reaction, and [A]0 keeps shrinking by half at every stage, each successive half-life is half as long as the one before it.
First half-life = 20.0 s; second half-life = 10.0 s (half as long, as expected for zero order)
P6Arrhenius Equation — Solving for Ea

k1 = 2.0×10−3 at T1 = 295 K; k2 = 8.9×10−2 at T2 = 350 K. Find Ea.

Strategy: Two (k, T) pairs signal the two-point Arrhenius equation. Rearrange to solve for Ea directly — no need to convert temperature units here since both are already in Kelvin. Watch your sign: the equation has a minus sign built in.
  1. Write the two-point Arrhenius equation:
    lnk2k1 = −EaR1T2 − 1T1
  2. Rearrange to isolate Ea:
    Ea = R·ln(k2/k1)1/T1 − 1/T2
  3. Compute the ratio k2/k1:
    k2k1 = 8.9×10−22.0×10−3 = 44.5
  4. Take the natural log: ln(44.5) = 3.795.
  5. Compute 1/T1: 1/295 = 3.390×10−3 K−1.
  6. Compute 1/T2: 1/350 = 2.857×10−3 K−1.
  7. Subtract to get the denominator:
    1T1 − 1T2 = 3.390×10−3 − 2.857×10−3 = 5.32×10−4 K−1
  8. Substitute everything into the Ea equation:
    Ea = (8.314)(3.795)5.32×10−4
  9. Compute the numerator, then divide: (8.314)(3.795) = 31.55; Ea = 31.55 / 5.32×10−4 = 59,300 J/mol.
Ea ≈ 59.2 kJ/mol
P7Arrhenius Equation — Solving for k at a New Temperature

Ea = 88.0 kJ/mol; k1 = 5.0×10−4 at T1 = 310 K. Find k2 at T2 = 340 K.

Strategy: Given Ea and one (k, T) point, solve the two-point equation for ln(k2/k1) first, then exponentiate to isolate k2. Convert Ea to Joules to match R = 8.314 J/mol·K.
  1. Convert Ea to joules to match R: Ea = 88.0 kJ/mol = 88,000 J/mol.
  2. Write the two-point Arrhenius equation:
    lnk2k1 = −EaR1T2 − 1T1
  3. Compute 1/T2: 1/340 = 2.941×10−3 K−1.
  4. Compute 1/T1: 1/310 = 3.226×10−3 K−1.
  5. Subtract: 1/T2 − 1/T1 = 2.941×10−3 − 3.226×10−3 = −2.85×10−4 K−1.
  6. Compute Ea/R: 88,000/8.314 = 10,585.
  7. Multiply to get ln(k2/k1):
    lnk2k1 = −(10,585)(−2.85×10−4) = 3.02
  8. Exponentiate both sides to remove the ln: k2/k1 = e3.02 = 20.4.
  9. Solve for k2: k2 = k1 × 20.4 = (5.0×10−4)(20.4).
k2 ≈ 1.0×10−2 (same units as k1)
P8Reaction Mechanism Analysis

Step 1 (slow): NO2Cl(g) → NO2(g) + Cl(g). Step 2 (fast): NO2Cl(g) + Cl(g) → NO2(g) + Cl2(g). Find (a) the overall balanced equation, (b) the molecularity of each step, (c) the reaction intermediate, and (d) the experimentally observed rate law for the overall reaction.

Strategy: Add the steps and cancel anything that appears as both a product and a reactant (that's your intermediate) to get the overall equation. Molecularity = count of reactant particles in EACH individual step. The rate law comes only from the slow (rate-determining) step, using its own coefficients as orders (valid since it's elementary).
  1. Add the two elementary steps together, keeping every species on the side it appears:
    NO2Cl + NO2Cl + Cl → NO2 + Cl + NO2 + Cl2
  2. Cancel any species that appears identically on both sides: Cl appears once on each side, so it cancels.
  3. Write the resulting overall equation:
    2NO2Cl(g) → 2NO2(g) + Cl2(g)
  4. Determine the molecularity of Step 1 by counting its reactant particles: only 1 (NO2Cl) → unimolecular.
  5. Determine the molecularity of Step 2 by counting its reactant particles: 2 (NO2Cl and Cl) → bimolecular.
  6. Identify the intermediate: Cl is produced in Step 1 and fully consumed in Step 2, and it does not appear in the overall equation → Cl is the intermediate.
  7. Identify the rate-determining step: the problem states Step 1 is slow, so Step 1 is rate-determining.
  8. Write the rate law using the rate-determining step's own coefficients as the reaction orders (valid because it's elementary):
    Rate = k1[NO2Cl]
Overall: 2NO2Cl(g) → 2NO2(g) + Cl2(g); Step 1 unimolecular, Step 2 bimolecular; intermediate = Cl; Rate = k[NO2Cl]
P9Reaction Energy Diagram

Ea(forward) = 62 kJ/mol; ΔHrxn = −35 kJ/mol. Determine (a) whether the reaction is exothermic or endothermic, (b) Ea(reverse), and (c) whether Ea(forward) or Ea(reverse) is larger, and whether that makes sense given the sign of ΔHrxn.

Strategy: The sign of ΔH tells you exo/endothermic directly. Use ΔHrxn = Ea(fwd) − Ea(rev), rearranged to solve for Ea(rev).
  1. Read the sign of ΔHrxn: it is negative (−35 kJ/mol) → the reaction is exothermic (products lower in energy than reactants).
  2. Write the relationship between ΔH and the two activation energies:
    ΔHrxn = Ea(fwd) − Ea(rev)
  3. Rearrange to solve for Ea(rev):
    Ea(rev) = Ea(fwd) − ΔHrxn
  4. Substitute the known values: Ea(rev) = 62 − (−35).
  5. Simplify the double negative and compute: Ea(rev) = 62 + 35 = 97 kJ/mol.
  6. Sanity-check the result: Ea(rev) = 97 kJ/mol is larger than Ea(fwd) = 62 kJ/mol. This makes sense: since the reaction is exothermic, the products sit in a lower energy well than the reactants, so climbing back UP from products to the transition state (the reverse reaction) must cross a bigger energy gap than going forward.
Exothermic; Ea(reverse) = 97 kJ/mol (larger than Ea(forward), as expected for an exothermic reaction)
Chapter 17 — Equilibrium
P10Writing K Expressions & Interpreting Magnitude

(a) Write the Kc expression for 2NO(g) + O2(g) ⇆ 2NO2(g). Given Kc = 6.0×105, does this equilibrium favor reactants or products? (b) Write the Kc expression for the heterogeneous equilibrium Ag2CO3(s) ⇆ 2Ag+(aq) + CO32−(aq).

Strategy: Products over reactants, each raised to its coefficient. Omit any pure solid or pure liquid. Compare K to 1: K » 1 favors products, K « 1 favors reactants.
  1. (a) Identify products and reactants of 2NO(g) + O2(g) ⇆ 2NO2(g): product is NO2 (coefficient 2); reactants are NO (coefficient 2) and O2 (coefficient 1). All species are gases, so none are omitted.
  2. Write Kc as products over reactants, each raised to its coefficient:
    Kc = [NO2]2[NO]2[O2]
  3. Compare the given Kc to 1: 6.0×105 is much greater than 1, so the equilibrium strongly favors products.
  4. (b) Identify the phases in Ag2CO3(s) ⇆ 2Ag+(aq) + CO32−(aq): Ag2CO3 is a pure solid; Ag+ and CO32− are dissolved (aqueous).
  5. Omit the pure solid from the expression (its "concentration" is constant and folded into K).
  6. Write Kc using only the aqueous species, each raised to its coefficient:
    Kc = [Ag+]2[CO32−]
    Note there is no denominator here at all — this is exactly why a "solubility product" expression has this form.
(a) Kc = [NO2]²/([NO]²[O2]); favors products. (b) Kc = [Ag+]²[CO32−]
P11Reaction Quotient (Q vs. K)

K = 170 for 2NO2(g) ⇆ N2O4(g). At some point, [NO2] = 0.025 M, [N2O4] = 0.040 M. Calculate Q. Is the reaction at equilibrium? If not, which direction will it proceed?

Strategy: Write Q using the exact same form as K, plug in the given (non-equilibrium) concentrations, then compare Q to K to determine direction.
  1. Write the reaction quotient in the same form as K:
    Q = [N2O4][NO2]2
  2. Substitute the given concentrations:
    Q = 0.040(0.025)2
  3. Compute the denominator: (0.025)2 = 6.25×10−4.
  4. Divide to get Q:
    Q = 0.0406.25×10−4 = 64
  5. Compare Q to K: Q (64) < K (170).
  6. Determine the direction: since Q < K, the reaction is not at equilibrium and must proceed toward products (right) to raise Q up to 170.
Q = 64; not at equilibrium; shifts right (toward N2O4)
P12Converting Kc to Kp

2SO3(g) ⇆ 2SO2(g) + O2(g), Kc = 4.08×10−3 at T = 1000 K. Calculate Kp at this temperature.

Strategy: Find Δngas (product gas moles minus reactant gas moles) from the balanced equation, then plug into Kp = Kc(RT)Δn using R = 0.0821 L·atm/mol·K.
  1. Count moles of gas on the product side: 2SO2 + O2 = 2 + 1 = 3 mol gas.
  2. Count moles of gas on the reactant side: 2SO3 = 2 mol gas.
  3. Compute Δngas = (product gas moles) − (reactant gas moles) = 3 − 2 = +1.
  4. Write the Kp/Kc relationship:
    Kp = Kc(RT)Δn
  5. Compute RT using R = 0.0821 L·atm/mol·K and T = 1000 K: RT = (0.0821)(1000) = 82.1.
  6. Substitute everything in:
    Kp = (4.08×10−3)(82.1)1
  7. Multiply to get the final answer: Kp = (4.08×10−3)(82.1).
Kp ≈ 0.335
P13ICE Table — Perfect-Square Shortcut

H2(g) + Br2(g) ⇆ 2HBr(g), K = 81.0. 3.00 L flask: 1.20 mol H2, 1.20 mol Br2, 0 mol HBr. Set up an ICE table and find the equilibrium concentration of each species.

Strategy: Convert moles to molarity first. Since [H2]0 = [Br2]0, the K expression becomes a perfect square — take a square root instead of using the full quadratic formula.
  1. Convert moles to molarity using the 3.00 L flask volume:
    [H2]0 = [Br2]0 = 1.20 mol3.00 L = 0.400 M
  2. Set up the ICE table (letting x be the drop in [H2] and [Br2]):
    H2Br22HBr
    Initial0.4000.4000
    Change−x−x+2x
    Equilibrium0.400−x0.400−x2x
  3. Write the K expression and substitute the equilibrium row:
    K = [HBr]2[H2][Br2] = (2x)2(0.400−x)(0.400−x)
  4. Recognize the perfect square (both denominator factors are identical) and rewrite as a single squared fraction:
    81.0 = 2x0.400−x2
  5. Take the square root of both sides — this is the shortcut that avoids the quadratic formula:
    √81.0 = 2x0.400−x   ⇒   9.00 = 2x0.400−x
  6. Cross-multiply to clear the fraction: 2x = 9.00(0.400−x).
  7. Distribute the right side: 2x = 3.60 − 9.00x.
  8. Collect the x-terms on one side: 2x + 9.00x = 3.60 → 11.00x = 3.60.
  9. Solve for x: x = 3.60/11.00 = 0.327 M.
  10. Back-substitute into the equilibrium row to get each concentration:
    [H2]eq = [Br2]eq = 0.400 − 0.327 = 0.0727 M   |   [HBr]eq = 2(0.327) = 0.655 M
  11. Check by plugging the equilibrium concentrations back into the original K expression:
    K = (0.655)2(0.0727)(0.0727) = 0.4290.00529 ≈ 81.1 ✓ (matches, within rounding)
[H2]eq = [Br2]eq = 0.0727 M; [HBr]eq = 0.655 M
P14ICE Table — Small-x Approximation

2SO2(g) + O2(g) ⇆ 2SO3(g), Kp = 1.0×10−3. PSO2 = 4.0 atm, PO2 = 2.5 atm, PSO3 = 0 initially. (a) Using the small-x approximation, estimate the equilibrium partial pressure of SO3. (b) Check whether the approximation was valid.

Strategy: Since Kp is small, assume x is small enough that (initial − x) ≈ initial for the reactants — this avoids a cubic/quadratic. ALWAYS check afterward that x is less than about 5% of the initial value.
  1. Set up the ICE table in pressures (letting 2x be the pressure of SO3 formed):
    2SO2O22SO3
    Initial4.02.50
    Change−2x−x+2x
    Equilibrium4.0−2x2.5−x2x
  2. Apply the small-x approximation to the reactant terms, since Kp is small: 4.0−2x ≈ 4.0 and 2.5−x ≈ 2.5.
  3. Write Kp using the approximated equilibrium row:
    Kp = PSO32PSO22·PO2 ≈ (2x)2(4.0)2(2.5)
  4. Substitute the given Kp and solve for (2x)2:
    (2x)2 = Kp × (4.0)2 × 2.5 = (1.0×10−3)(16)(2.5) = 0.040
  5. Take the square root: 2x = √0.040 = 0.200.
  6. Solve for x: x = 0.200/2 = 0.100 atm.
  7. Compute the equilibrium pressure of SO3: PSO3 = 2x = 0.20 atm.
  8. Check the approximation's validity: compare x to the smaller initial pressure (O2 = 2.5 atm):
    xPO2,0 = 0.1002.5 = 0.040 = 4.0%
    Since 4.0% is under the usual 5% cutoff, the approximation is valid.
PSO3 ≈ 0.20 atm; approximation valid (x is 4.0% of the limiting initial pressure)
P15Calculating Kp from Equilibrium Pressures

N2(g) + 3H2(g) ⇆ 2NH3(g). At equilibrium: PN2=0.50 atm, PH2=0.75 atm, PNH3=0.20 atm. Calculate Kp for this reaction.

Strategy: When you're already given equilibrium pressures directly, just plug straight into the Kp expression — no ICE table needed.
  1. Write the Kp expression, products over reactants, each raised to its coefficient:
    Kp = PNH32PN2·PH23
  2. Substitute the equilibrium pressures:
    Kp = (0.20)2(0.50)(0.75)3
  3. Compute the numerator: (0.20)2 = 0.0400.
  4. Compute (0.75)3: 0.75 × 0.75 × 0.75 = 0.4219.
  5. Multiply to get the full denominator: (0.50)(0.4219) = 0.2109.
  6. Divide numerator by denominator:
    Kp = 0.04000.2109
Kp ≈ 0.190
P16Le Châtelier's Principle — Multiple Reactions

Predict the shift when volume is decreased (pressure increased) for four reactions, plus a temperature question for a fifth.

Strategy: Count total moles of GAS ONLY on each side. Compressing (raising pressure) always shifts toward the side with FEWER gas moles; if both sides have equal gas moles, there's no shift. For temperature, treat heat as a reactant (endothermic) or product (exothermic).
  1. (a) 2NO(g) + O2(g) ⇆ 2NO2(g): count reactant gas moles (2+1 = 3) vs. product gas moles (2). Fewer moles on the product side → compressing shifts right.
  2. (b) H2(g) + CO2(g) ⇆ H2O(g) + CO(g): count reactant gas moles (1+1 = 2) vs. product gas moles (1+1 = 2). Equal on both sides → no shift.
  3. (c) N2O4(g) ⇆ 2NO2(g): count reactant gas moles (1) vs. product gas moles (2). Fewer moles on the reactant side → compressing shifts left.
  4. (d) CaCO3(s) ⇆ CaO(s) + CO2(g): count GAS moles only — reactant side has 0 mol gas (CaCO3 is a solid); product side has 1 mol gas (CO2). Fewer moles on the reactant side → compressing shifts left (favors more solid CaCO3).
  5. (e) 2SO2(g) + O2(g) ⇆ 2SO3(g) is exothermic — rewrite it with heat as a product: reactants ⇆ products + energy. Raising temperature is like adding more of that "product" (energy), so the equilibrium shifts left (toward reactants), and K decreases.
(a) right   (b) no shift   (c) left   (d) left   (e) shifts left; K decreases
