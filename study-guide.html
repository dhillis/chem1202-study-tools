Chapter 16, Part 1 — Reaction Rates

Chemical kinetics is the study of reaction rates and the factors that affect them. Reaction rate is the change in the concentration of a reactant or product per unit time.

Factors that increase reaction rate

  • Higher concentration — more reactant particles means more collisions per second.
  • Higher temperature — particles move faster, so they collide more often and with more energy.
  • Greater surface area (for solids) — more particles exposed for collision (classic example: a steel nail glows feebly in O2, but the same mass of steel wool bursts into flame).
  • A catalyst — provides a lower-energy pathway (see 16.6).

Relating the rates of different species

For a A + b B → c C + d D, every species' rate of change must be divided by its own coefficient (reactants get a minus sign, since they're disappearing) so all four expressions equal the same single "rate of reaction":

rate = −(1/a)Δ[A]/Δt = −(1/b)Δ[B]/Δt = (1/c)Δ[C]/Δt = (1/d)Δ[D]/Δt

Example: for H2(g) + I2(g) → 2HI(g), [HI] forms twice as fast as [H2] disappears, purely because of the 2:1 mole ratio — not because of any difference in "speed."

Chapter 16, Part 2/3 — Rate Laws & the Method of Initial Rates

The rate law for a A + b B → products is Rate = k[A]n[B]m, where:

  • k = the rate constant (specific to a reaction at a given temperature)
  • n, m = reaction orders, determined only by experiment — never assume they equal the balanced-equation coefficients a and b (they sometimes coincide, but there's no rule that they must).
  • Overall reaction order = n + m.

The "doubling trick" for finding an order

Run two trials where only ONE reactant's concentration changes. If that concentration is doubled and the rate changes by some factor, solve 2order = (rate factor) for the order — this is an exponent equation, so you cannot just multiply or divide the ratio directly.

If doubling [A] causes the rate to......then the order in A is
Not change at all (×1)0 (zero order): 20 = 1
Double (×2)1 (first order): 21 = 2
Quadruple (×4)2 (second order): 22 = 4
Increase by a non-obvious factorUse logs: n = log(rate ratio) / log(conc. ratio)

A zero-order reactant's concentration drops out of the rate law entirely (since anything0 = 1). The units of k always depend on the overall order, since the whole rate law must come out in M/s (or M/time).

Half-life patterns (memorize the direction of each)

OrderHow each successive half-life compares to the one before it
Zero orderHalved each time (100 → 50 → 25 hours, e.g.) — because t½ is directly proportional to [A]0, which keeps shrinking.
First orderConstant — t½ = ln2/k does not depend on [A]0 at all. This is why radioactive decay (always first order) has a single, fixed half-life.
Second orderDoubles each time (100 → 200 → 400 hours) — t½ is inversely proportional to [A]0, which keeps shrinking, so each half-life takes longer.
Carbon-14 dating relies on first-order decay: because t½ doesn't depend on the starting amount, the half-life (≈5,730 years) is the same no matter how much carbon-14 you start with.

Chapter 16, Part 4 — Collision Theory & the Arrhenius Equation

Collision theory: particles must collide to react, and an "effective" collision requires two things at once:

  1. Enough energy to clear the activation energy (Ea) — the energy threshold needed to reach the transition state.
  2. The correct molecular orientation — the reactive atoms must actually make contact.

Raising temperature increases rate mainly because it increases the fraction of collisions with energy ≥ Ea (Ea itself doesn't change) — plus it modestly increases collision frequency.

The Arrhenius equation

k = A·e−Ea/RT

A (the frequency factor) = p·Z, the product of collision frequency (Z) and the orientation-probability factor (p). Two-point form, used when you know k at two temperatures:

ln(k2/k1) = −(Ea/R)(1/T2 − 1/T1)     R = 8.314 J/mol·K

Always convert T to Kelvin and Ea to Joules (to match R). Because k depends on T exponentially, a modest % rise in T (or a modest % drop in Ea from a catalyst) produces a disproportionately larger % change in rate — never a matching linear change.

Reaction energy diagrams

ΔHrxn = Ea(forward) − Ea(reverse)

The transition state (activated complex) sits at the energy maximum of each step and cannot be isolated. In a multi-step mechanism, each elementary step has its own transition state and its own little "hump" on the diagram — the number of humps = the number of elementary steps.

Chapter 16, Part 5 — Reaction Mechanisms

  • A mechanism is the sequence of elementary steps that add up to the overall balanced equation.
  • Molecularity = the number of reactant particles in ONE elementary step (unimolecular = 1, bimolecular = 2, termolecular = 3 — rare, since a 3-body simultaneous collision is unlikely).
  • For an elementary step only, reaction order = molecularity, so you CAN read the rate law straight off its coefficients. This does not apply to the overall multi-step reaction.
  • An intermediate is produced in an early step and consumed in a later one — it cancels out when you sum the steps and never appears in the overall equation or its rate law.
  • The rate-determining step is the slowest elementary step. Its rate law (using its own coefficients) becomes the rate law for the entire reaction.

Checking whether a proposed mechanism is valid

  1. The elementary steps must sum to the overall balanced equation (intermediates cancel).
  2. Each step must be physically reasonable (generally ≤2, occasionally 3, reactant particles).
  3. The mechanism must correlate with the experimentally observed rate law (via the rate-determining step).
A mechanism is a hypothesis, not a proof — meeting all three criteria makes it a reasonable, useful model, not a certainty.

Chapter 16, Part 6 — Catalysts

  • A catalyst speeds up a reaction by providing an alternative pathway with a lower overall activation energy — it is not consumed.
  • It speeds up the forward and reverse reactions equally, so it does not change ΔHrxn, the equilibrium position, or K. It only gets the system to equilibrium faster.
  • Homogeneous catalyst: same phase as the reactants (e.g., Br−(aq) catalyzing H2O2(aq) decomposition). Heterogeneous catalyst: different phase (e.g., a solid metal catalyzing a gas-phase hydrogenation).
  • Enzymes are biological catalysts. The substrate binds to the enzyme's active site — the lock-and-key model treats the site as a fixed shape; the induced-fit model has it flex to bind more effectively.

Chapter 17, Part 1 — The Equilibrium Constant, K

At equilibrium, rateforward = ratereverse, and concentrations of reactants/products stay constant (not necessarily equal — just unchanging). This is a dynamic state: both reactions keep happening, just at matched rates.

For a A + b B ⇆ c C + d D:

Kc = [C]c[D]d / [A]a[B]b

Products over reactants, each raised to its coefficient as an exponent — never as a multiplier. Pure solids and pure liquids are always omitted (their "concentration" is constant and gets folded into K).

Value of KWhat it means
K « 1 (e.g., 2.00×10−3)Reactants favored — little product forms
K ≈ 1Neither side favored — comparable amounts of both
K » 1 (e.g., 200)Products favored — reaction goes nearly to completion

K for a reversed or scaled reaction

  • Flipping a reaction: Kreverse = 1/Kforward
  • Multiplying every coefficient by n: Knew = (Koriginal)n — never K × n
  • Adding two reactions in series: Koverall = K1 × K2

Chapter 17, Part 2 — Reaction Quotient (Q vs. K)

Q has the exact same form as K, but is evaluated at any point in the reaction, not just at equilibrium.

ComparisonWhat happens
Q < KToo little product relative to equilibrium → reaction shifts right (toward products) until Q rises to K
Q > KToo much product relative to equilibrium → reaction shifts left (toward reactants) until Q falls to K
Q = KAlready at equilibrium — no net change
No reversible reaction at equilibrium goes to 100% completion (that requires K = ∞) or stays at 0% conversion (K = 0) — some finite, nonzero amount of reactant is always converted, but never all of it.

Chapter 17, Part 3 — Kc and Kp

For gas-phase equilibria, K can be written with partial pressures (Kp) instead of concentrations (Kc):

Kp = Kc(RT)Δngas     R = 0.0821 L·atm/mol·K
  • Δngas = (moles of gaseous product) − (moles of gaseous reactant) — only count gas-phase species; solids, liquids, and aqueous species are ignored, same as in the equilibrium expression itself.
  • If Δngas = 0, then Kp = Kc exactly.
  • Use R = 0.0821 L·atm/mol·K here (not 8.314 J/mol·K, which is for Arrhenius/thermodynamics problems) since pressures are in atm.

Chapter 17, Part 4 — ICE Tables & Solving Equilibrium Problems

An ICE table tracks a species' amount through three rows: Initial, Change (usually ±x scaled by stoichiometric coefficients), and Equilibrium (Initial + Change).

The sign of x depends on which way the reaction must shift from the given starting point — if you start with zero product, the reaction must run forward (products get +x, reactants get −x), regardless of which side is written as "reactants."

Three common solving patterns

  1. Direct algebra: if the equilibrium amount of one species is given outright, solve for x directly — no quadratic needed.
  2. Perfect-square shortcut: when both reactants start at equal concentration and only one product forms, the K expression often reduces to a perfect square (e.g., K = [2x/(c−x)]²) — take a square root instead of using the quadratic formula.
  3. Small-x approximation: when K is small relative to the initial concentrations, only a little reactant converts to product, so x is small enough that (initial − x) ≈ initial. This avoids a quadratic, but you must check afterward that x is less than about 5% of the initial value — if not, you must solve exactly (see next point).
A quadratic always gives two mathematical roots, but only one is physically valid. Reject any root that would make an equilibrium concentration negative — concentrations can never be less than zero.

Chapter 17, Part 5 — Le Châtelier's Principle

"If a system at equilibrium is disturbed, it shifts in the direction that partially relieves the disturbance."

DisturbanceResponse
Add reactantShifts right (toward products); consumes some of the added reactant
Remove reactantShifts left (toward reactants); replaces some of what was removed
Add productShifts left (toward reactants)
Remove productShifts right (toward products)
Decrease volume (increase pressure)Shifts toward the side with fewer total moles of gas
Increase volume (decrease pressure)Shifts toward the side with more total moles of gas
Equal gas moles on both sidesPressure/volume changes cause no shift

Temperature — treat heat as a reactant or product

Reaction typeRaising temperatureLowering temperature
Exothermic (heat is a product: A+B ⇆ C+D+energy)Shifts left; K decreasesShifts right; K increases
Endothermic (heat is a reactant: energy+A+B ⇆ C+D)Shifts right; K increasesShifts left; K decreases

Only temperature changes actually change the value of K. Concentration and pressure/volume changes shift the position of equilibrium but K itself stays the same.

Ammonia synthesis trade-off (straight from lecture notes): N2 + 3H2 ⇆ 2NH3 is exothermic and slow. Raising temperature speeds up the rate (kinetics) but shifts equilibrium toward reactants, hurting the yield (equilibrium). Improving one can worsen the other.
Catalysts do not shift equilibrium. They speed up the forward and reverse reactions equally, so equilibrium is reached faster — but the equilibrium position and K are unchanged. Don't confuse "gets there faster" with "makes more product."

Chapter 16 must-knows

  • Determine the rate of reaction from concentration data, and know why coefficients >1 require dividing (the "−1/2" in front of a rate expression).
  • Identify the parts of the rate law equation: what are n, m, and k? How is the order of a reactant determined?
  • Use the method of initial rates to determine reaction orders.
  • Use the integrated rate law equations to find an unknown (e.g., concentration at time t) — the professor will give you the equations, but you must know which is which.
  • Verify a mechanism: do the steps add up to the overall reaction? What role does an intermediate play?
  • What does a catalyst do, and how does it speed up a reaction?
  • Label reaction energy-profile diagrams and determine endothermic vs. exothermic.
  • What is an enzyme? What is Q and what is half-life, in relation to K?

Chapter 17 must-knows

  • What is chemical equilibrium? Write the equilibrium expression for a given chemical equation (including a heterogeneous one, like NH3(g) + HCl(g) ⇆ NH4Cl(s)).
  • Know what the value of K means: does K = 200 favor products or reactants? Does K = 2.00×10−3?
  • Construct and use an ICE table to solve equilibrium problems (the quadratic formula will be given if needed).
  • What effect does a catalyst have on equilibrium?
  • Le Châtelier's Principle: know how equilibrium shifts (and which side is favored) for adding/removing reactant or product, raising/lowering temperature for exo- and endothermic reactions, and increasing/decreasing pressure for gas-phase reactions.

Common Traps Seen Across the Homework

  • Reaction orders (n, m) are never assumed equal to balanced-equation coefficients — only true for a genuinely elementary step. Always get orders from experimental data.
  • When solving "concentration doubled, rate changed by factor X" problems: set up 2order = X and solve for the exponent. Do not multiply or divide the ratio directly — that's the #1 trap in the Method of Initial Rates.
  • First-order half-life does not depend on starting concentration; zero- and second-order half-lives do (and change in opposite directions as the reaction proceeds).
  • Arrhenius/Ea problems require T in Kelvin and Ea in Joules to match R = 8.314 J/mol·K — mismatched kJ vs. J is the most common error.
  • An intermediate is produced then consumed (cancels from the overall equation); it should never appear in the final experimental rate law.
  • The rate law for the overall reaction comes from the slowest (rate-determining) step only — not from the overall balanced equation's coefficients.
  • Pure solids and liquids are dropped from BOTH the equilibrium expression and the Δngas count for Kp/Kc conversion.
  • Kreverse = 1/Kforward (never −K); scaling all coefficients by n gives Kn (never K×n).
  • In an ICE table, reject any root of a quadratic that produces a negative equilibrium concentration.
  • Catalysts speed up how fast equilibrium is reached but never change K or the equilibrium position — a very common exam trap, tested in both Ch 16 and Ch 17.
  • Only temperature changes the value of K. Concentration and pressure/volume changes shift the equilibrium position but leave K unchanged.
  • For pressure/volume Le Châtelier questions, count total moles of gas only on each side — solids and liquids don't matter for this rule.
